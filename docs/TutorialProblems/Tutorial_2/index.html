    Tutorial 2: Compaction via a rigid body¶

    Introduction¶

    This quick start tutorial introduces the concept of a rigid body interacting with the material points. It builds on Tutorial 1 and introduces the concept of a rigid body interacting with the material points.

    This tutorial analyses a cube of soil compressed by a rigid cube by 25% of its height. It is used to validate that the contact formulation produces the expected uniform vertical stress and displacement fields. This problem has been used to validate our contact formulation 1 and our adaptive-octree extension 2.

    This tutorial has three main sections after the introduction:

    • Input setup
    • Deploying and running the problem
    • Viewing the results

    Background: rigid-body contact¶

    This tutorial extends Tutorial 1 to include normal contact. A brief overview is provided here for context; see equilibrium with rigid-body contact for the full technical details. Figure 1 provides a schematic overview of how the contact between the rigid body and the material points works.

    When contact is detected between a GIMP and the rigid body, (a) initial state, a normal contact force is applied at the corners of the GIMP's domain to resist the overlap. This force is proportional to the amount of overlap and can be thought of as a spring whose stiffness resists the overlap. MPM3S calculates the spring stiffness automatically from the GIMP size and material properties,

    \[ \epsilon_N = 50\, E_p\, A_p^0, \]

    where \(E_p\) is the smallest Young's modulus of the GIMPs in the element and \(A_p^0 = (V_p^0)^{2/3}\) is a representation of the contact area, with \(V_p^0\) the initial GIMP volume. A tangential stiffness of \(25\, E_p\, A_p^0\) is used for friction. The springs are stiff but not rigid, so a small overlap between the rigid body and the GIMPs remains - a few millimetres in this problem.

    After the springs have been activated the GIMPs and the mesh deform due to the contact forces created by the springs, (b) deformed state, and once convergence is obtained the mesh is reset, (c) mesh reset.

    Input setup¶

    Problem summary¶

    The aim is to introduce the set-up and modelling of soil-structure interaction problems. The problem is a deformable cube compressed by a rigid cube. Although simple, it is essential, as it validates the contact formulation by confirming that the overlap between the two bodies stays small and that the resulting vertical stress field is uniform throughout the cube and matches the analytical Hencky solution for the Cauchy stress in the vertical direction

    \[ \sigma_{zz} = E \ln\!\left(\frac{L}{L_0}\right) \frac{L_0}{L} = \frac{10^6}{0.75} \ln(0.75) \approx -3.84 \times 10^5 \text{ Pa}, \]

    where \(L_0 = 0.8\) m is the initial cube height, \(\Delta z = -0.2\) m is the prescribed compression and \(L = L_0 + \Delta z = 0.6\) m is the final height after the \(25\%\) axial compression.

    As in Tutorial 1 the material is homogeneous Hencky elastic, here with \(E = 10^6\) Pa, \(\nu = 0\) and \(\rho = 1000\) kg/m\(^3\). The whole \(1.6\) m background domain is discretised by a uniform \(0.4\) m mesh (\(4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64\) elements); the soil fills \(2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8\) of them, with a \(2 \times 2 \times 2\) grid of GIMPs per element. Roller boundaries are imposed on the four side faces and the base, and the top face is left free for the rigid cube to push on.

    The rigid cube is a \(1.2 \times 1.2 \times 1.0\) m box, centred over the soil, that overhangs its top by \(0.2\) m on every side. It starts resting on the soil and is pushed down \(0.2\) m over 20 load steps.

    Gravity is switched off, so all of the load on the soil comes from the rigid cube.

    The simulation is configured through a single JSON object - a human-readable, editable text file. It extends Tutorial 1 with a rigid body. The complete file for this problem, and the rigid cube geometry, can be found here, and every key is described on the input_data.json file format page.

    input_data_compress.json

    Machine and domain¶

    "GPU": "off" runs the analysis on the CPU.

    "domain update": "stretch" lets the GIMP domains to compress as the cube is compressed. Without it, the domains keep their initial size, and the GIMPs next to the base cannot move closer to it.

    The background mesh is sized automatically to hold the soil and the same height of empty space above it, so it is a cube of side \(1.6\) m. "gravity" is zero, so the soil carries no weight.

    "GPU": "off",

"domain update": "stretch",

"domain": {
    "gravity": [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
}

    Material points¶

    The soil is one \(0.8\) m layer on a \(0.8 \times 0.8\) m footprint, discretised by \(0.4\) m elements with \(2 \times 2 \times 2\) GIMPs in each, using the elastic properties from the Problem summary.

    "material points": {
    "extra capacity": 1.2,
    "element size": 0.4,
    "number of material points per element 1": 2,
    "material size": { "min": [0.0, 0.0], "max": [0.8, 0.8] },
    "layers": [
        {
            "thickness": 0.8,
            "material": { "type": "elastic", "E": 1.0e6, "nu": 0.0, "density": 1000.0 }
        }
    ]
}

    Rigid body¶

    A rigid body is a system of one or more points. Each point has six degrees of freedom - three displacements and three rotations - and carries a mass and rotational inertia. The rigid cube is a single point at its centre, \((0.4, 0.4, 1.3)\) m.

    Its "boundary conditions" list the six degrees of freedom in the order \([u_x, u_y, u_z, \theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z]\). Five are "fixed", and the vertical displacement is prescribed as \(-0.01\) m per load step, so the rigid cube moves \(0.2\) m down over the 20 load steps. Because every degree of freedom is held, the mass and inertia have no effect.

    The geometry comes from cube.stl, which sits in the same folder as the input file: the "stl" path is relative to the input file, so only file name means the same folder. It is attached to point 1 and drawn in global coordinates (the same global coordinates as point 1) with its base on the top of the soil. On the first run MPM3S builds a tetrahedral mesh of the rigid cube and saves it in cube_mesh.txt, which later runs read instead. If you change cube.stl, delete cube_mesh.txt so that the mesh is rebuilt.

    "rigid bodies": [
    {
        "points": [
            { "position": [0.4, 0.4, 1.3], "mass": 1.0, "rotational inertia": [0.2, 0.2, 0.24],
              "boundary conditions": ["fixed", "fixed", { "prescribed": -0.01 }, "fixed", "fixed", "fixed"] }
        ],
        "stl files": [
            { "name": "cube", "stl": "cube.stl", "mesh cache": "cube_mesh.txt", "point": 1 }
        ]
    }
]

    Contact¶

    The contact between the rigid cube and the soil is frictionless. The contact stiffness is set automatically, as described in the Background.

    "contact": {
    "friction coefficient": 0.0
}

    Analysis¶

    A single static stage of 20 load steps with the rigid body switched on. Gravity is zero, so "load": "full" has nothing to apply; all of the load comes from the rigid cube, which is pushed down a little further at every step.

    "rigid body surface placement": "on" moves the rigid cube vertically at the start of the stage so that its base rests exactly on the top of the soil. Rollers are applied on the four sides and the base, and the top is free.

    "adaptivity": { "type": "uniform" } meshes the whole background domain at the \(0.4\) m element size. Without it, only the elements holding material points are \(0.4\) m and the empty space is filled with coarser elements.

    "analysis": [
    {
        "type": "static",
        "load steps": 20,
        "load": "full",
        "rigid bodies": "on",
        "rigid body surface placement": "on",
        "boundary conditions": { "min": ["roller", "roller", "roller"], "max": ["roller", "roller", "free"] },
        "adaptivity": { "type": "uniform" }
    }
]

    Solver¶

    The same settings as Tutorial 1: Newton-Raphson iterations to a tolerance of \(10^{-6}\), as there are no small ovelaps in this example there is no need for ghost stabilisation.

    "solver": {
    "tolerance": 1.0e-6,
    "max newton iterations": 20,
    "poor factor": 0.25,
    "ghost factor": 0.0,
    "ghost factor mass": 0.0
}

    Output¶

    VTK and CSV output at every load step. The CSV files record each GIMP's position, domain size ("lp") and stress, and the rigid cube's position and reaction force - the force needed to push it - which are used in Analysing the stress in the domain.

    "output": {
    "vtk": "on",
    "vtk directory": "vtk_cube",
    "vtk percent": 0,
    "vtk material point fields": ["displacement", "stress", "strain"],
    "vtk rigid body fields": ["position", "reaction force"],

    "csv": "on",
    "csv directory": "csv_cube",
    "csv percent": 0,
    "csv material point fields": ["initial position", "position", "lp", "stress"],
    "csv rigid body fields": ["position", "reaction force"]
}

    Deploying and running the problem¶

    MPM3S is written in the Julia programming language, and there are two ways to run the code, both explored on the deployment page. As this is a small problem that runs quickly, this tutorial uses Julia directly; see the installation guide for installing Julia and MPM3S.

    Working in the MPM3S folder

    Julia runs in the MPM3S folder - the folder you downloaded - throughout this tutorial, and every path in the commands and scripts is relative to it. The input file is read from validation_data_and_jsons/input_data_compress.json, and the output folders vtk_cube and csv_cube are written to, and read back from, the MPM3S folder.

    Deployment instructions
    terminal

    Setting up and running the problem¶

    The input file for this tutorial comes with MPM3S, as validation_data_and_jsons/input_data_compress.json, together with the geometry of the rigid cube, cube.stl, and its mesh cache, cube_mesh.txt; they are the same files as the ones provided here. The STL and mesh-cache paths in the input file are relative to the input file, so the files must stay in the same folder.

    As in Tutorial 1, open a terminal, start Julia with every CPU thread available (-t auto), then move into the folder where you downloaded MPM3S, activate it and load MPM3S. The output folders vtk_cube and csv_cube are created in the MPM3S folder.

    $ julia -t auto

julia> cd("path/to/MPM3S")
julia> using Pkg; Pkg.activate(".")
  Activating project at `path/to/MPM3S`

julia> using MPM3S

    Running the problem¶

    Julia is now running in the MPM3S folder and MPM3S loaded. The JSON file for this problem is loaded with MPM3S and is stored in the folder validation_data_and_jsons, the simulations are started with:

    MPM3S.non_linear_solve("validation_data_and_jsons/input_data_compress.json");

    Reading the output¶

    Before the first load step, MPM3S reports the rigid body surface placement: the lowest point of the rigid cube (body base) and the top of the soil (material top) are both at \(0.8\) m, so the rigid cube is moved by just Δz = 2.23517e-8 m, a small tolerance above the soil.

    The progress line then works as in Tutorial 1. The load fraction t rises by Δt 0.05 per load step, each step converges in 4 Newton-Raphson iterations (NR), and no steps are cut or material points deleted. The uniform background mesh has N 125 nodes, a \(5 \times 5 \times 5\) grid.

    julia> MPM3S.non_linear_solve("validation_data_and_jsons/input_data_compress.json");
[ Info: rigid body surface placement: body base 0.8 → material top 0.8   Δz = 2.23517e-8
stage 1/1 ██░░░░░░░░░░  15.0% t 0.15/1.0 step 4 Δt 0.05 NR 4 cuts 0 del 0 N 125

    Viewing the results¶

    The simulation results appear as the simulation runs, so you do not need to wait until it has finished. vtk_cube holds three series of files, numbered from 00002 to 00021: mps_1_...vtu for the GIMPs, surface_...vtu for the surface of the rigid cube and body_...vtu for the rigid cube's point which contains information such as force and moment reactions.

    Visualising the output in ParaView¶

    The walkthrough below follows the same pattern as the ParaView walkthrough from Tutorial 1, but also loads the surface of the rigid cube and makes it see-through, so that the soil beneath it can be inspected. The same 3D-navigation controls (left-drag to rotate, scroll to zoom) apply throughout.

    1. Open ParaView. Launch ParaView from your applications menu or terminal; you should see an empty render view.

    2. Open the output files. Use File → Open and navigate to vtk_cube. ParaView groups the numbered files into three series: body_..vtu, mps_1_..vtu and surface_..vtu. Hold Ctrl and click mps_1_..vtu and surface_..vtu so both are highlighted, then click OK.

    3. Apply the readers. Click Apply in the Properties panel. Both series appear in the Pipeline Browser, and the render view shows the rigid cube resting on the soil. The rigid cube overhangs the soil and hides it from most directions, so zoom out and rotate the view to see both bodies.

    4. Make the rigid cube see-through. Select surface_..vtu in the Pipeline Browser and set Opacity, under Styling, to 0.6. The soil can now be seen through the rigid cube, which overhangs it on every side.

    5. Choose a field to colour the GIMPs by. Select mps_1_..vtu and open the Coloring menu, which lists the fields written to the VTK files: displacement, strain and stress. Choosing displacement → Z and stepping through time shows the displacement of the GIMPs increasing evenly from the base to the top of the soil as the rigid cube moves \(0.2\) m down.

    6. Inspect the final stress distribution. Change the GIMPs' Coloring to stress → ZZ, click Go to Last (▶|) and then Rescale to Data Range. The soil has been compressed to about \(0.6\) m under the rigid cube. Additionally to inspect the contact force select surface_..vtu and change its Coloring to contact force → Magnitude. The force is zero over the sides and top of the rigid cube and is carried by the triangles of its base, which is in contact with the soil.

    Analysing the stress in the domain¶

    csv_cube/rigid_body.csv has one row per load step for the rigid cube's point, body1. The time column is the load fraction, body1_position_z is the height of the rigid cube's centre and body1_reaction force_z is the vertical force needed to push the rigid cube down, in N:

    time,body1_position_x,body1_position_y,body1_position_z,body1_reaction force_x,body1_reaction force_y,body1_reaction force_z
0.05,0.4,0.4,1.290000022351742,0.0,0.0,8022.186499840163
0.1,0.4,0.4,1.280000022351742,0.0,0.0,16343.582579162323
...
1.0000000000000002,0.4,0.4,1.1000000223517419,0.0,0.0,237097.96125687487

    The GIMP files, csv_cube/mps_1_00002.csv to mps_1_00021.csv, have the same layout as in Tutorial 1, with the addition of the domain half-widths lp_x, lp_y and lp_z.

    The short Julia script below compares the final state with the analytical solution from the Problem summary. As in Tutorial 1, you can run it in the same Julia session straight after the analysis, either by copying it into the REPL or by saving it as check_stress.jl in the MPM3S folder and running include("check_stress.jl"). It needs only DelimitedFiles, which comes with Julia.

    using DelimitedFiles

# the soil at the final load step; skip the "time" line, then read the header
soil, soil_header = readdlm("csv_cube/mps_1_00021.csv", ',', Float64; skipstart=1, header=true)
soil_column(name) = soil[:, findfirst(==(name), vec(soil_header))]
z    = soil_column("position_z")
lp_z = soil_column("lp_z")
szz  = soil_column("stress_zz")

# the rigid cube: one row per load step, so the last row is the final step
rigid, rigid_header = readdlm("csv_cube/rigid_body.csv", ',', Float64; header=true)
rigid_column(name) = rigid[end, findfirst(==(name), vec(rigid_header))]
force_z = rigid_column("body1_reaction force_z")
base_z  = rigid_column("body1_position_z") - 0.5    # the rigid cube is 1 m tall

E, L0, area = 1.0e6, 0.8, 0.8 * 0.8
L = maximum(z + lp_z)                                # final height of the soil
println("final soil height       ", round(L, digits=4), " m (overlap ", round(1000 * (L - base_z), digits=1), " mm)")
println("mean vertical stress    ", round(sum(szz) / length(szz) / 1000, digits=1), " kPa")
println("Hencky at that height   ", round(E * log(L / L0) * L0 / L / 1000, digits=1), " kPa")
println("rigid cube force / area ", round(-force_z / area / 1000, digits=1), " kPa")

    For this run it prints:

    final soil height       0.6046 m (overlap 4.6 mm)
mean vertical stress    -370.5 kPa
Hencky at that height   -370.5 kPa
rigid cube force / area -370.5 kPa

    The rigid cube overlaps the soil by \(4.6\) mm - about \(0.6\%\) of the soil's height - so the soil is compressed to \(0.6046\) m rather than \(0.6\) m. At that height the Hencky solution gives \(-370.5\) kPa, matching the mean stress in the GIMPs; the full \(0.2\) m of compression would give the \(-383.6\) kPa of the Problem summary, \(3.5\%\) more.

    The stress is uniform: every GIMP has the same vertical stress, \(-370.5\) kPa, and the rigid cube force divided by the area of the soil gives the same value.

    To see the effect of the mesh, set "element size" in Material points to 0.2 and compare the overlap and the stresses.
